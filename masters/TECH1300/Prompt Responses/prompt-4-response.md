### Guide for developing the Selection section

**1. Explain the purpose of the matrix**

Begin by explaining that the selection matrix translates the outcomes of planning into measurable criteria.

Planning identifies the organisation’s needs, risks and priorities. Selection then uses these findings to compare possible systems in a structured and transparent way.

The matrix should answer:

> Which system is the best fit for this organisation’s current requirements and future strategy?

**2. Define the main selection criteria**

Organise the matrix into five areas:

* critical business requirements;
* integration capability;
* user experience and adoption;
* vendor or implementation-partner suitability;
* product roadmap and future fit.

Do not give every criterion the same importance. Critical requirements and integration may receive higher weights because failure in these areas can prevent the system from supporting essential business processes.

**3. Identify what evidence is required**

The organisation should not rely only on vendor claims. For each criterion, identify evidence that the supplier must provide.

Examples include:

* demonstrations based on real business scenarios;
* API documentation;
* customer references;
* service-level agreements;
* training and onboarding materials;
* security information;
* the product roadmap;
* expected implementation costs and time.

This makes the decision more evidence-based and reduces the influence of marketing.

**4. Use a simple scoring method**

Give each alternative a score, such as one to five, and multiply the score by the criterion’s weight.

However, explain that the final score should support rather than automatically determine the decision. A numerical result may hide important risks or dependencies.

**5. Separate mandatory and desirable requirements**

Before calculating the weighted score, classify requirements as:

* **Must-have:** essential and non-negotiable;
* **Should-have:** important but not essential;
* **Could-have:** desirable additional value.

A system that fails a must-have requirement may need to be removed from consideration, even when it receives a strong total score.

Examples include regulatory compliance, protection of sensitive data or integration with a critical legacy system.

**6. Develop the integration criterion**

Do not ask only whether the system provides APIs.

Use IBM’s integration approaches to evaluate different needs:

* APIs for exposing data and functions;
* application integration for connecting workflows and existing applications;
* messaging for reliable asynchronous communication;
* events for allowing systems to respond to changes in real time.

The matrix can therefore evaluate:

* compatibility with legacy and cloud systems;
* API security and documentation;
* support for messaging or event-driven integration;
* reliability;
* scalability;
* monitoring and governance;
* data consistency.

The main point is that no integration method is best in every situation. The selected approach should match the organisation’s technical and business requirements.

**7. Add critical interpretation**

Explain that the system with the largest number of features is not automatically the best option.

A solution may offer advanced functionality but also require excessive customisation, training or architectural complexity. Therefore, the organisation should select the smallest combination of capabilities that adequately satisfies its requirements.

**8. Connect selection with the other stages**

Finish by showing the relationship between the stages:

* **Planning** defines needs and priorities.
* **Selection** converts them into criteria and evaluates alternatives.
* **Implementation** puts the selected solution into operation.

A weak selection process may create problems that implementation teams cannot easily solve later.

### Suggested section order

1. Explain the purpose of requirements-based selection.
2. Introduce the five criteria in the matrix.
3. Explain weights, evidence and scoring.
4. Discuss mandatory requirements.
5. Develop the integration criterion using IBM.
6. Critically explain the limitations of numerical scoring.
7. Connect selection with planning and implementation.

### Review question

After writing the section, check:

> Have I only described a selection matrix, or have I explained how it improves the quality and transparency of the decision?
