### Response

For a High Distinction, the report must go beyond description. It should demonstrate exceptional clarity, critical analysis, strong application to the chosen application, compelling evidence, insightful evaluation, and precise referencing and presentation.

Give me a guide for developing this section at a High Distinction standard, including advice on academic English.

2. **Application to Transperth:** Demonstrate what occurred during Empathise, Define, Ideate, Prototype and Test, using evidence about new and infrequent passengers rather than merely defining the stages.

For academic English, the guidance was to use explicit analytical links such as “This finding informed…”, “Consequently…”, and “This decision is expected to…”. Claims should follow an evidence-to-decision-to-impact pattern and avoid unsupported expressions such as “this design is better.”

## Interaction 16 - Understanding Kuriakose and Sandnes (2024)

### Prompt

> Can you explain the reference stating that accessibility evaluation should combine interface audits with testing involving diverse users, using more accessible language?

### Response

Kuriakose and Sandnes examined a Norwegian public-transport application in two complementary ways. First, they conducted a technical accessibility review involving areas such as contrast, forms, navigation order and notifications. Second, they tested the application with six users with different characteristics, including people with disabilities.

The technical audit identifies possible violations of accessibility guidance, while user testing reveals how those problems affect people performing real tasks. For the Transperth project, the prototype could first be checked against selected WCAG 2.2 criteria and then tested by users with different needs while they plan a journey or interpret a disruption. Because the study involved another application and only six participants, it supports the proposed evaluation method but does not prove that Transperth has the same accessibility problems.

## Interaction 17 - Evaluating UX design principles and practices

### Prompt

> Let us move to “Evaluation of UX Design Principles and Practices.” The assessment requires evaluation of the effectiveness of the principles and practices applied to the application, critical analysis of their impact on user experience and application success, and evidence-based examples and recommendations for future enhancements.

### Response

Approximately 500-600 words were recommended, focusing deeply on three principles rather than describing many principles superficially:

1. **Visibility of system status and feedback:** The application distinguishes live and scheduled services, but the acknowledged 15-45 second update delay may create false confidence when data freshness is not visible. Timestamps, plain-language status explanations and recovery actions were recommended.
2. **Error prevention and recovery:** App-store feedback suggests possible search and journey-state problems. Preserving active journeys, displaying contextual error messages, retaining entered information and offering alternatives could reduce repetition and cognitive effort.
3. **Accessibility and inclusive design:** Relevant areas include contrast, touch-target size, plain language, labelled icons, screen-reader-compatible status messages and error identification. Specific WCAG 2.2 criteria should be applied, but full compliance should not be claimed without a complete audit.

The overall evaluation should connect these principles to trust, task completion and continued use. Future work should include testing with international students, infrequent passengers and users with accessibility needs, using measures such as completion rate, time, errors, comprehension and confidence.

For every principle, the recommended analytical sequence was: identify the principle, present evidence, judge its effectiveness, explain its impact, recommend an improvement and define how improvement would be measured.
